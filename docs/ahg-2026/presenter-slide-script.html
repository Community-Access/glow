Slide 2. Forty thousand files and three people | 10:31-10:33

Words to say — the slide and its context:

“Imagine forty thousand files and three people responsible for accessibility. How many of you recognize that kind of backlog? And how many have a spreadsheet with ‘final’ in its name more than once?” Pause; repeat one or two contributions into the microphone.

“The federal Title II web accessibility rule identifies WCAG 2.1 AA as its technical standard for covered public entities. The Department of Justice currently lists April 26, 2027 for state and local government entities serving populations of 50,000 or more, and April 26, 2028 for smaller entities and special district governments. The applicable date depends on the public entity, so check the current guidance for your institution. Many materials involved are documents: syllabi, slide decks and scanned readings. A small team cannot solve an ever-growing backlog simply by working harder. Our question is: how do we help more people do this work without losing quality, accountability or trust?”

Slide 13. Automation helps. You decide. | 11:00-11:03

Words to say — the slide and its context:

“Automation helps; you decide. An agent can read a whole course quickly, but you still need to know what accessible work looks like. It can explain a checker’s finding, but you must compare its claim with the actual evidence. It can draft fixes and plans, but a person decides what goes out and signs off. It can cite a standard, but a person must notice what the checker did not see and check whether the citation supports the claim.

“A confident answer is not proof. Good instructions and evidence can help good work scale; careless instructions can scale mistakes. Never call something compliant just because an agent says so. Before a finding reaches a student or faculty member, read it and verify it.”

Slide 16. Maria's six answers | 11:07-11:17

Words to say — the slide and its context:

“Maria’s role is an alternate-format planner. Its task is to plan formats from checker reports. Its trusted guidance includes WCAG 2.2 and the ACB Large Print Guidelines. Its output is numbered steps identifying who does each one. Its example starts with a scanned reading and leads to large print. Its human review is a specialist checking the plan.

“Those are the short answers. Now I will show you how to turn each into an instruction you could actually use. As I enter them, listen for the input, the expected result and the person who checks it.”

Say: "Watch me first. Maria works in a fictional disability resource center. I will answer each question as Maria; then you will answer for your own task."

Show: Run /design-my-agent in Copilot Chat, or use the block 2 browser text. Follow the assistant's actual prompts; if it phrases or orders them differently, identify each of the six answers as it arises. Read the following aloud and enter the corresponding answer:

  1. Role — say: "Who is this agent, and who does it work for? Maria's answer is: 'You are an alternate-format planner working for a disability resource specialist.' This gives the assistant a perspective and tells it who needs the result. It does not mean the assistant is a real specialist."
  2. Task — say: "What one job does it do, and what does it need? Maria says: 'Given a checker report and a generic format requirement, draft a plan for producing those formats.' The report and requirement are inputs; the plan is the output. Keeping the task narrow makes it easier to test."
  3. Trusted guidance — say: "What should it use and cite? Maria names WCAG 2.2, including 1.4.5 and 1.1.1 where relevant, the ACB Large Print Guidelines and her center's approved procedure. Naming a source gives the reviewer somewhere to check a claim. It does not make every citation correct: we still verify that the criterion actually applies and that the procedure can be used in the approved assistant."
  4. Output format — say: "What should come back? Maria wants a short problem statement, numbered steps with responsible roles, the formats to deliver and a review checklist. That shape helps someone act on the draft; a long general essay would be harder to hand off."
  5. Example — say: "What does a useful answer look like? For a scanned reading, first obtain clean text, then check it against the original, and only then prepare large print and audio. The example teaches the shape and order of a good answer. It is fictional, not evidence that the actual file was checked."
  6. Human review — say: "Who checks what? The alternate-format specialist checks the converted text against the original, and the student confirms that the format works for them. Naming a role and a specific check turns 'a human reviews it' into a real handoff."

If the demonstration fails — say: "The assistant did not complete that step. We can still use the written Maria example; the six answers we just discussed are the instructions we wanted it to assemble." Open Maria's full example, or use the saved kit copy if offline.
